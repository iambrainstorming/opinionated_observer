# History Will Not Be Kind: Delhi Police and the Duty to Disobey Unlawful Orders

Date: 23-10-2026

When a police force is seen siding with power against the public it serves, the damage goes beyond a single protest. It erodes the trust on which law and order depends. Amid recent protests about election rigging in the capital, Delhi Police has faced that allegation, and the questions being asked deserve serious answers.


## The Right That Needs No Permission

The Constitution is explicit. Article 19(1)(a) protects freedom of speech and expression, and Article 19(1)(b) protects the right to assemble peacefully and without arms. Indian courts have repeatedly held that dissent is not a threat to democracy but part of how it works.

A peaceful assembly does not become lawful because an official approves it. It is lawful because the Constitution says so. The police's role is to facilitate it, manage traffic, and prevent violence from any quarter, not to decide whether citizens deserve to be heard.


## What Protesters and Rights Groups Allege

Protesters, lawyers, and civil liberties organisations have made a series of serious allegations about the policing of demonstrations in Delhi:

- Use of pellet guns and tear gas against crowds.
- Lathi charges on unarmed, non-violent demonstrators.
- Mass detentions, including of women held late at night, which the law restricts.
- Allegations of misconduct against women like groping.
- FIRs filed on thin grounds, with protesters kept in jail for weeks, in some cases for little more than raising the national flag.

These are allegations, and each needs independent, transparent investigation. But the pattern as described, force against the unarmed and custody as a weapon of deterrence, is exactly what the Constitution was written to prevent.

## Section 163 and the Blanket Ban

Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, which replaced Section 144 of the CrPC, allows prohibitory orders in urgent cases of apprehended danger. In *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020), the Supreme Court made clear that such orders cannot be used to suppress legitimate expression, must be proportionate, and must state reasons and be open to judicial review.

In Delhi these orders have become a routine tool: imposed over wide areas, applied in advance, and used to declare lawful gatherings illegal. When a safeguard meant for emergencies becomes a standing excuse, the exception swallows the right.


## "Orders From Above"

The most common defence is that officers are simply following instructions. The principle behind it is ancient: a soldier or officer cannot be blamed for obeying a lawful command. But the same principle has a limit. An order that is manifestly illegal offers no protection to those who carry it out. Striking peaceful, unarmed citizens, detaining women at night against procedure, or arresting people without cause are not grey areas.

Conscientious refusal does not mean mutiny. In practice it looks like:

- declining to use force on peaceful, unarmed people;
- asking for lawful, written directions;
- recording and reporting illegal orders through proper channels;
- upholding every procedural safeguard for people in custody, especially women.

Officers across India have done this quietly, often at personal cost. They are the ones who kept faith with their oath.

## Whose Money, Whose Uniform

Police salaries, vehicles, and equipment are paid for by public taxes, including those paid by the protesters themselves. The authority an officer holds is delegated by the public, not owned by the government of the day. When that authority is turned against the people who granted it, the institution becomes a kind of hired force rather than a public service.

## The Family Question

Every officer goes home to a family. The question critics pose is simple: what would your parents, your spouse, or your children think of what they saw on camera? The person on the receiving end of a lathi is someone's mother, someone's son. Institutions are made of individuals, and individuals have to live with what they did.


## The Verdict of History

Records survive. Videos circulate, court orders accumulate, and memories endure. Societies eventually judge their institutions by what they did when pressure came to abandon the public. History has treated those who defended the law at a hard moment with respect, and those who enforced injustice, whatever their orders, with something closer to shame.

## Conclusion 

Delhi Police has a choice: to be remembered as Dyer — who opened fire on a large, unarmed crowd gathered for the Baisakhi festival, firing approximately 1,650 rounds over ten minutes in the Jallianwala Bagh massacre — or to be remembered as a true patriot of the country and to stand with the people who are fighting to stop election rigging and make the process fully transparent.
